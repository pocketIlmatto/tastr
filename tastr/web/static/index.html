        Glossary

        What each label, number and pattern in Tastr means, and how the app works it out. Everything here is measured from the audio file alone, not from metadata, genre tags or online lookups. (Optional catalog lookups are shown separately, as "Listed elsewhere".) The measurements are good starting points for making music, not a transcription.

        Track basics

        BPM (tempo)

        How fast the beat goes, in beats per minute.

        • Found from note onsets and the kick drum. Within 4% of the labeled tempo on 87% of Beatport tracks and 72% of GiantSteps tracks.
        • "95% likely right" means: of benchmark tracks whose beat looked this steady and clear, 95% got the right tempo. Most tracks read about 94%; very unsteady ones can drop to 10%. (Older cards show a raw "conf" score until re-analyzed.)
        • A track can still read at half or double speed (87 instead of 174), so both are kept as alternatives.
        Bar and downbeat

        The downbeat is beat 1 of each bar. Bars are 4 beats (4/4) or 3 beats (3/4, waltz time), shown as "time" at the top of the card.

        • The time signature comes from how the beats group: whether sound, bass and drums repeat every 3 beats or every 4. On labeled songs it finds about 2 in 3 waltzes and calls about 1 in 27 songs in 4/4 a waltz by mistake; when unsure it stays 4/4.
        • If it's wrong, pick "Change to 3/4 time" or "Change to 4/4 time" in the track's ⋯ menu. Re-analyzing keeps your choice. 6/8 and songs that change meter are read as 3/4 or 4/4.
        • Beat 1 is right 64% of the time on 4/4 songs; most misses are half a bar off.
        Key

        The home note and mode (major or minor) the track fits best.

        • From a small model trained on GiantSteps and Beatport EDM Key.
        • Exact on 60% of GiantSteps and 49% of Beatport.
        • Each track's key shows its own "likely right" figure the same way (about 30% to 86%).
        • The usual miss is a key a fifth away, which still mixes fine.
        Camelot (e.g. 8A)

        A circle that organizes musical keys so you can quickly find tracks that mix in key.

        • Each key gets a number (1-12) and a letter: A is minor (inner ring), B is major (outer ring).
        • Safe moves: stay on the same code, step one number up or down on the same letter, or switch letters on the same number.
        • Mixed In Key also describes bolder moves, like the Energy Boost (two numbers up, 8A to 10A). Matching keys only shortlists tracks; still check the transition by ear.
        • Learn more: How to use the Camelot Wheel · Harmonic mixing guide (Mixed In Key)

        Scale (mode)

        Which set of notes the track uses on top of its key.

        • Modes are scales built from the same notes as the major scale, each starting on a different note. There are seven; Tastr picks from six, plus Harmonic Minor (a scale, not a mode). The table below lists them.
        • Tastr puts it to work in two places. The Ableton setup tip names the root and scale to set in Live's Scale control, so the piano roll highlights the notes that fit. The harmony suggestions give a "safe" lead scale: the 5-note pentatonic version of the key, which leaves out the notes most likely to clash with the chords.
        • Learn more: Ableton Learning Music (interactive, start with Notes and Scales) · musictheory.net lessons (Scales and Modes) · Mode (Wikipedia)

        Loudness
        The average level of the whole file in dB (RMS). Mastered club tracks sit around −8 to −12 dB.

        Energy

        Tastr has two kinds of energy, and they answer different questions.

        Where you see itWhat it meansHow it is worked out
        Track energy (the 0-1 number and meter at the top)How loud, busy and intense the whole track is, comparable between tracks.A weighted mix: 35% loudness (−30 dB maps to 0, −8 dB to 1), 25% note-onset rate (6 onsets per second or more counts as full), 20% brightness and 20% percussiveness.
        Energy chart (Energy & sections)How energy rises and falls inside this track, from bar to bar.The average loudness (RMS in dB) of each bar, scaled so the track's quietest 5% of bars read 0 and its loudest 5% read 1. Because it is relative, a quiet ambient track still uses the full height of the chart.
        Section energyThe mean of the chart over a section's bars. It decides the section labels.Same 0-1 relative scale as the chart.

        Vibes

        Each vibe gets a score from 0 to 1 from fixed rules over the measurements below. Vibes scoring under 0.20 are hidden; Show all scores under the vibes lists those between 0 and 0.20. A vibe that scores 0 or less, or whose measurements are missing, isn't listed at all. Vibes describe the sound, not a genre.

        drivingRhythmStrong, steady pulse at a dance tempo.115-150 BPM only: (pulse clarity − 0.73) ÷ 0.35

        Tempo hints (group Tempo) score a flat 0.5 when the BPM falls in a style's usual range.

        • They are hints, not genre calls.
        • Ranges overlap, so a track can get more than one: 140 BPM gets techno, dubstep/halftime and trap tempo, and 125 BPM gets house and techno tempo.
        • "Halftime" means the snare lands on beat 3 instead of 2 and 4, so a 140 BPM dubstep or trap track feels like 70 (see Drum styles below).

        Measurements behind vibes

        Danceability is fitted to listener labels. Energy and the vibe rules' weights and cut-offs are hand-picked, but benchmarked against MTG-Jamendo listener labels (3,069 tracks) and DEAM listener ratings (1,802 clips), Oct 2026. Percentages are the chance that a labeled track ranks above an unlabeled one (50% is a coin flip):

        • Danceability vs "danceable": 82%, vs "party": 80% (was 70% and 68% before the fit). The danceable tag is right on 72% of the tracks it shows on, where 38% would be chance.
        • Energy vs "aggressive": 85%, and it follows how energetic listeners rate a clip (DEAM arousal, correlation 0.67).
        • dark and melancholic track how negative listeners rate a clip, bright how positive (DEAM valence, about ±0.45).
        • driving and hypnotic aren't validated yet, and atmospheric shows on too many tracks.
        Brightness (0-1)
        The average spectral centroid (the "center of mass" of the frequencies) divided by 3.5 kHz. Higher means more treble.
        Percussiveness (0-1)
        The share of the track's energy in its percussive part after splitting the audio into harmonic (sustained) and percussive (short, sharp) layers.
        Low end (0-1)
        The share of the spectrum's power below 150 Hz.
        Pulse clarity (0-1)
        How much stronger the onsets on the beat are than the average onset. A clear four-on-the-floor kick scores high; a beatless pad scores near 0.
        Danceability (0-1)
        75% percussiveness, 25% how close the tempo is to about 135 BPM (the fit fades over about ±45 BPM). Fitted on MTG-Jamendo tracks against listeners' "danceable" and "party" labels (Oct 2026). Pulse clarity turned out not to help, so it isn't used.
        Swing % and confidence
        Where the off-beat 16th lands within each beat: 50% is straight, about 67% is a full triplet shuffle. Confidence is how consistent that position is from beat to beat.
        Section contrast
        The spread (standard deviation) of the section energies. High contrast means big builds and drops.

        Sections

        Finding the boundaries. For each bar Tastr measures its harmony (12-note chroma), timbre (MFCCs) and loudness. Bars are then grouped into runs that sound alike (temporally constrained clustering), aiming for roughly one section per 18 seconds (between 3 and 12). Boundaries snap to 2-bar phrases, and anything shorter than 2 bars merges into its neighbor. Tracks under 8 bars get one "full" section. The 18 seconds and 2 bars were tuned on 912 labeled pop songs (the Harmonix Set), where Tastr's boundaries score 58% (F-score, a hit is within 3 seconds of a labeled boundary).

        Letters. Sections that sound alike share a letter: "Drop C" and a later "Drop C" are the same material coming back, while "Build B" after "Intro A" is new material. Section colors in the chart and tabs follow the letter, so repeats share a color. The boundary confidence is how different a section sounds from the one before it.

        Labels come from each section's energy relative to the rest of the track, checked in this order:

        LabelWhat it usually isRule
        IntroThe opening, before the track gets going.First section, energy under 0.5
        OutroThe ending, winding down (often DJ-friendly drums).Last section, energy under 0.5
        DropThe peak of an electronic track, where the full beat and bass hit.Energy 0.68 or more, in an electronic track (over 105 BPM and percussiveness over 0.25)
        ChorusThe peak of a song that isn't drum-led.Energy 0.68 or more, not electronic
        BuildThe rise into a peak: risers, snare rolls, filters opening.The next section is 0.6 or more and over 0.2 higher
        BreakdownA stripped-back dip, often without kick or bass.Energy under 0.38
        VerseEverything in between: a steady middle part.None of the above

        Labels are guesses from loudness alone. A quiet chorus can read as a verse, and section edges can be 1-4 bars away from where a part really comes in.

        Drum styles & pattern ideas

        The Pattern picker in the Drums part offers ready-made patterns next to the detected groove. Tastr picks a style from the tempo only; it does not check whether the track's actual rhythm matches. Lanes map to the default Drum Rack pads.

        StyleChosen whenWhat it is
        Four on the floor118-135 BPMA kick on every beat (all four "on the floor"), claps or snares on 2 and 4, off-beat hats. The backbone of house, techno and disco.
        Halftime136-152 BPM and percussiveness over 0.25The snare moves to beat 3, so a fast tempo feels half as fast. Dubstep, trap and halftime DnB.
        Drum and bass160-185 BPMA fast, syncopated kick and snare (the two-step), often built from chopped breakbeats like the Amen break.
        Hip-hop80-104 BPMA laid-back, syncopated kick with a backbeat snare on 2 and 4; boom bap or trap-style hats.
        Generic backbeatAny other tempoKick on 1 and 3-ish, snare on 2 and 4. The basic rock and pop beat.

        How parts are detected

        Drums, bass and chords are read from a 16-step grid: each bar is split into 4 beats and each beat into 4 sixteenths. Patterns are measured per section, averaged over the section's bars.

        Steps: a filled step (x) is a hit at least 72% as strong as the lane's loudest; a soft step (o) is at least 50%; anything quieter is empty (.).

        Bass rhythmWhere bass notes startJumps in low-frequency energy at each of the 16 steps, averaged over the section's bars with bass.-
        ChordsOne chord per barEach bar's 12-note pitch profile is matched against all 24 major and minor triads. A match scoring under 0.6 counts as unsure and shows as "—". Only plain major and minor triads are recognised, so a 7th or sus chord reads as its nearest triad.-

        How the hits are separated. The drums are first split from the sustained sounds. That percussive layer is divided into low, mid and high parts (a learning method called NMF), the bleed between them is measured and subtracted for each track, and every hit is sorted into a lane by its pitch, length and texture. A lane is shown only when it has a clear, repeating pattern, and quieter lanes (clap, open hat, tom, perc) also need at least 20% of the loudest lane's level.

        Bass movement
        Pedal: one note over 70% of the time. Stepwise: mostly moves by 1-2 semitones. Fourths/fifths: mostly jumps of 5 or 7 semitones (classic root motion). Leaps: bigger, mixed jumps. Static: never changes.
        Loop (bass or chords)
        The 4-bar window that repeats most often in the section. If the section is shorter than 4 bars, its first bars.

        Curious how it works under the hood? Audio analysis terms explains the signal processing behind these measurements (spectrograms, chroma, onsets, tempograms and more), with diagrams.

        Other terms

        Roman numerals (i, VI, III, VII)
        Each chord's position in the key: uppercase is major, lowercase is minor. In A minor, i-VI-III-VII is Am-F-C-G. Numerals let you move a progression to any key.
        Scale degree
        A note's position in the scale, counted from the tonic (1).
        Detected vs template
        Detected patterns are measured from this track. Templates are pattern ideas chosen by tempo, not by listening.
        Your edits
        Changes you make to drums, bass or chords, highlighted in the edit color. They save automatically, and Versions keeps named snapshots.
        Δ (delta)
        "Δ Build B" on a bar means it differs from the same bar in the previous section, Build B. It is not an edit. "Outline Δ from the previous section" outlines every step, note and chord that differs.
        Sidechain
        Ducking the bass (or pads) each time the kick hits so the kick cuts through. Tastr suggests a release time from the track's 8th-note length.
        Warp mode
        How Live stretches audio to a new tempo: Beats for drum-heavy material, Complex Pro for full mixes and vocals. Tastr suggests one from the percussiveness.
        Listed elsewhere
        BPM and key from online catalogs (GetSongBPM, FreqBlog, AcousticBrainz), found by artist and title. Off unless you turn them on; a mark shows whether each agrees with what Tastr measured. They never change the measured values.
        Stems
        The track split into drums, bass, vocals and other (with the optional Demucs install), run on demand per track.
        Waveform and spectrogram
        The waveform shows loudness over time. The spectrogram shows which frequencies sound when: low at the bottom, bright at the top. The Live tab draws the spectrogram as the track plays, either scrolling (2D) or as a 3D landscape of ridges, one per moment of sound. ⤢ Expand shows any chart bigger, or full screen.

        Your files

        Everything Tastr stores for you: the audio you uploaded and the stems made from it. Stems are made only when you ask, and you can delete them to free space and make them again later.
